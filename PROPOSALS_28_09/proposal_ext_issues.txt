The following are the critics of the proposal.

1. Tangent Prop / Tangent Distance (Simard, LeCun & Denker, NeurIPS 1991; Simard, LeCun, Denker & Victorri 1993) — this is closer prior art than Lucas-Kanade for your headline contribution. Your Section 5 geometric operator is: estimate the Jacobian of a known transform via finite differences, then project a feature-space difference onto the orthogonal complement of that Jacobian's span. That is exactly tangent distance/tangent prop's core operation — historically applied to project out rotation/shear/thickness tangent directions for handwritten-digit recognition. You currently cite Lucas-Kanade (1981), which is the right precedent for estimating displacement from image gradients, but the wrong precedent for projecting a representation onto/off a transformation's tangent subspace — that's Simard et al. A sharp reviewer will say "this is tangent distance on ViT tokens" whether or not you say it first; better to say it first, since it actually strengthens your framing (you're extending a 1991 idea from raw pixels to frozen foundation-model tokens, per-patch, for an unsupervised bitemporal setting — a defensible, citable lineage rather than an unexplained coincidence).

2. DRIFT (Ameta et al., Samsung Research, arXiv:2606.06918, June 2026) — this is your closest scoop risk, not NFAD. It's frozen-VFM, one-class, and for AI-generated-image detection rather than change detection, but the machinery is close enough that you must cite and differentiate it explicitly: it decomposes frozen VFM representation space into a "robust" subspace trained to suppress drift under physically-plausible imaging transformations and a "fragile" subspace sensitive to edit-like perturbations, with patch-wise drift used for localization. That is your radiometric-subspace idea (robust/fragile split) plus your nuisance-share map (patch-wise drift signature) in a different domain. It postdates NFAD by weeks and predates your proposal by about three months — exactly the kind of thing that turns "novel combination" into "someone already generalized this" if you don't cite it. The differentiator you can legitimately claim: DRIFT learns its subspaces (lightweight projection heads, one-class supervision); yours is fully training-free and closed-form (Jacobian + eigendecomposition), and yours is the only one with a local, per-patch geometric construction rather than a single global subspace. Say that explicitly.

3. CNN/ViT shift-invariance failure literature — needed to motivate H1, not just test it blind. Azulay & Weiss, "Why Do Deep Convolutional Networks Generalize So Poorly to Small Image Transformations?" (2019) and Zhang, "Making Convolutional Networks Shift-Invariant Again" (BlurPool, ICML 2019) are the standard references establishing that small shifts do move deep features non-trivially and non-smoothly — this is the reason your operator is needed at all, and it's also the reason H1 might fail (aliasing in early conv/patchify stages can make the shift response non-linear even at sub-pixel scale). Also worth a look: Darcet et al., "Vision Transformers Need Registers" (2023) — DINOv2/DINOv3-family artifact tokens could interact with your per-patch Jacobian estimation in patches near those artifacts; worth at least a footnote on whether you filter or account for register/artifact tokens.

4. Classical remote-sensing radiometric/geometric normalization — the actual operational incumbent, and currently absent from your table. Pseudo-invariant-feature radiometric normalization (Schroeder et al. 2006), IRMAD (Canty & Nielsen, 2008), and precise sub-pixel orthorectification/co-registration are what commercial EO change-detection pipelines already use today to suppress exactly this class of false alarm, in pixel/radiance space rather than deep feature space. This is the most important omission for the industry conversations you're about to have, because the first question a GEOSAT or Xoople engineer will ask is "why not just fix this in preprocessing the way we already do?" You need a paragraph that (a) names this baseline, (b) explains that NullShift operates downstream of it as a model-space complement (catches residual sub-pixel/radiometric mismatch that survives preprocessing, and works without retraining when a new sensor joins the constellation before a new normalization pipeline is calibrated for it), and (c) ideally adds classical relative radiometric normalization as a seventh baseline alongside your existing five.

One feasibility risk not in your table: DINOv3's positional embeddings may not interpolate perfectly smoothly under the sub-pixel shifts you use to estimate J(p) via finite differences — if position-embedding interpolation itself is a source of non-smoothness, your four-shifted-copies estimate of J could be noisy independent of whether the underlying visual features are locally linear. Worth a quick diagnostic (compare J estimated from shifts of {0.25, 0.5} px vs {1, 2} px — if they disagree a lot, suspect the position embedding rather than the visual backbone).

The per-patch geometric subspace is still probably your best original contribution — nobody has built a numerically-estimated, per-patch Jacobian-of-shift projection specifically for bitemporal RS change detection on a frozen satellite-pretrained ViT — but "moderate–good" should now read as conditional: it's moderate-good if you explicitly position it against Tangent Prop/Distance and DRIFT in the paper; if a reviewer finds those connections that you didn't make, it reads as derivative rather than a considered extension.

The underlying pain point is genuine and expensive: every commercial EO change-monitoring product loses credibility (and money, via analyst QA time or client disputes) when a co-registration or radiometric artifact gets reported as a real-world event. That's already partially solved today by classical preprocessing (sub-pixel orthorectification, PIF/IRMAD radiometric normalization) — so NullShift isn't filling an empty niche, it's offering a cheaper, retraining-free, foundation-model-native complement to an existing pipeline, plus a standardized way to quantify residual sensitivity as a number a company can put in a spec sheet.
